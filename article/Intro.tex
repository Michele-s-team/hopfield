\section{Introduction}


 In October 2024, John J. Hopfield was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics alongside Geoffrey Hinton for their
pioneering work on artificial neural networks \cite{nobel2024physics}. Their work laid the foundations for the development and understanding of neural networks, whether biological or artificial, and eventually influenced the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI).

Hopfield worked on an artificial neural network originally introduced independently by Shun'ichi Amari and Kaoru Nakano in 1972 \cite{Amari1972LearningPA,Nakano1972}, and by William A. Little in 1974 \cite{Little1974}. He greatly contributed to the analysis, understanding and popularization of the network in his landmark paper \textit{Neural networks and physical systems with emergent collective computational abilities} published in 1982 \cite{Hopfield1982}, so that the model was eventually named after him. The model, described in more detail in subsection \ref{subsec:hopfield_model}, has similarities with Ising or spin glass models: it is composed of a set of spins -- called neurons in the case of the Hopfield network -- $S_i \in \{-1, 1\}$ interacting with one another. Similarly to the Ising and spin glass models, the Hopfield model is also energy-based, its dynamics is an energy minimization process. What makes this model different from the other two models is that the interactions are quenched variables built from the patterns to be stored in the network. These patterns are attractors of the dynamics, which correspond to states of minima of the energy.

The Hopfield model provided the first simple yet powerful framework for understanding networks of interconnected neurons. It has become a reference in statistical mechanics on networks \cite{Mezard2017}. In a second paper, Hopfield showed that the same energy-based, content-addressable memory survives when the binary neurons are replaced by continuous-valued neurons with graded (sigmoidal) responses evolving in continuous time, making the model directly realizable as an analog electronic circuit and closer to biological neurons \cite{Hopfield1984}.

Then, Amit et al. solved the equilibrium statistical mechanics of the Hopfield model, identifying retrieval, mixture and spin-glass states and the phase diagram in the ($T$, $\alpha$) plane with the storage capacity $\alpha_c \simeq 0.138$ at $T = 0$ \cite{Amit1985,AMIT1987,Amit1989,Sompolinsky1988} (see also \cite{Hertz1991} for a pedagogical introduction). Derrida, Gardner and Zippelius \cite{Derrida1987} solved the dynamics of a strongly diluted, asymmetric Hopfield network exactly. They showed that a stored pattern is still retrieved, up to a capacity of $2/\pi$ per connection at zero temperature. Note that this is significantly higher than the capacity of the fully connected Hopfield network. They also show that the attractor near a stored pattern is not a single fixed configuration but a more complex attractor or a cloud of attractors. The same year 1987, E. Gardner introduced an important generalization of the Hopfield model, which includes interactions with more than 2 Ising spins \cite{Gardner1987b}. For this model, she obtained the exact storage capacity for large $N$, which grows much faster with $N$ than in the pairwise Hopfield model, where it is proportional to $N$.

The dynamics of the Hopfield model has been studied analytically both for a finite number of patterns and near saturation (see Ref.~\cite{Coolen2001} for a review), but it remains a challenging problem, which is why numerical simulations are often used to study the dynamics of the Hopfield model. However, simulating the dynamics of the Hopfield model is computationally demanding, especially when one wants to sample a large number of configurations. This is particularly true when one wants to study rare events in the dynamics or when one wants to sample a large number of initial conditions.

In this work, we implement a bitwise version of the Metropolis algorithm that allows us to efficiently simulate the dynamics of the Hopfield model by sampling several replicas of the system in parallel. The idea of storing several Ising variables in the bits of a single computer word is not new. This technique, known as multi-spin coding, was introduced in the early 1980s for Monte Carlo simulations of Ising systems \cite{JacobsRebbi1981,Zorn1981} and was rapidly combined with vector architectures \cite{Bhanot1986}. In its \emph{multisample} variant, each bit of a word belongs to a different realization of the system rather than to a different site of the same lattice. This is especially well suited to spin glasses, where many independent disorder samples must be simulated anyway, and it has been a standard tool in large-scale simulations of the Edwards--Anderson model \cite{Ogielski1985,HFreund_1988,palassini1999}, up to dedicated hardware such as the Janus computer \cite{Janus2009}. In these simulations, a single random number is commonly shared by all the samples encoded in a word, since generating random numbers is often the most expensive part of the update. This recycling of random numbers is acceptable as long as the samples differ through their disorder and initial conditions, so that the same random number leads to different outcomes in each sample; we discuss this condition, and the independence of the realizations it produces in our implementation, in Sec.~\ref{subsec:independence}. The bitwise implementation we use here belongs to this family of methods. Multi-spin coding has also been applied to neural networks: Neschen \cite{Neschen1991} developed an efficient multi-spin coding algorithm for Hopfield-type networks to store the couplings. Here, instead, we use multisample coding, in which each bit encodes an independent replica of the Hopfield network with its own patterns, and all replicas share the same random numbers. Because the Hopfield couplings are integer-valued rather than binary and the interactions are dense, the bitwise arithmetic is substantially more involved than for the Ising and spin glass models, and whether this approach pays off is precisely the question we address.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{subsec:hopfield_model} introduces the Hopfield model and its main equilibrium properties. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the Metropolis algorithm and the two optimizations we propose: a bitwise encoding of the spins, which evolves $64$ replicas of the system in parallel, and the sharing of random numbers across these replicas; it then details the standard and bitwise implementations on the Hopfield network, including an overlap-based formulation adapted to dense networks. Section~\ref{sec:results} discusses the independence of the realizations, tests the retrieval capacity of the network, illustrates its dynamics from random initial states and compares the performance of the bitwise and standard implementations. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with perspectives.
