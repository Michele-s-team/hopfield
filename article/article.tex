\title{When does multi-spin coding pay off for simulating sparse or dense spin glasses and Hopfield networks?}

\author{Bastien Dumont}
 \affiliation{Aix-Marseille Universit\'e, Marseille, France}
 \affiliation{Universit\'e Paris-Saclay, Orsay, France}

\author{David Lacoste}
 \affiliation{Gulliver Laboratory, CNRS UMR 7083, ESPCI Paris, PSL Research University, Paris, France}

\author{Michele Castellana}
 \affiliation{Institut Curie, PSL Research University, CNRS UMR 168, Paris, France}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The Hopfield model, introduced in its now-canonical form by J.J. Hopfield in 1982, laid the foundation for an understanding of neural networks, whether they are biological or artificial. In practice, such systems typically have a complex high-dimensional free energy landscape, and the dynamics of such systems is challenging to simulate computationally. For this reason, it is essential to optimize any algorithm used to simulate such systems. In this work, we implement a bitwise Metropolis algorithm and show that its implementation can efficiently sample 64 copies of a binary system. To benchmark the method, we test it with classical network models, such as the Ising model and the Edwards-Anderson model. Then we apply the method to the dense Hopfield network for which we design a specific version of the algorithm. While the method does not provide significant gain for the dense network, we find that it gives significant gain for the diluted network, which is more biologically plausible with a higher capacity per synapse.
\end{abstract}
